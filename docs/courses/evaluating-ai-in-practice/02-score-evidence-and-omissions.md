## What you will learn

Use a small rubric consistently and record why an output passed or failed.

## An omission can be an error even when every sentence is true

A rescheduling notice states the correct new room. It leaves out the closed entrance, so someone using that entrance cannot act on it.

Score required information as well as accuracy of stated claims. Use the same requirement list for manual and assisted drafts. Record why a reviewer rejected the output and whether the source itself was incomplete.

### Score the notice before revising it

The required facts are the new room, the time, and the only usable entrance. The draft says “Room 12, 6 p.m.” The organizer’s update says “Room 12, 6 p.m., enter through the south door.”

| Check | Result | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Correct location | Pass | The draft and source both say Room 12. |
| Correct time | Pass | Both say 6 p.m. |
| Entrance usable | Fail | The instruction a participant needs is missing. |

Two of three checks pass. A person who cannot reach the room will still experience the notice as a failure. Revise the notice, then record the failure in the trial so a later comparison includes how often it happens.

Apply the same check to the manual draft. It is easy to compare a tool's mistakes with an ideal version of manual work. Use evidence from the actual baseline.

## How it works

Define the evidence before reading results. Use observable checks such as correct date, source supports the central claim, and unresolved details remain visible. One serious failure can outweigh several correct cosmetic details.

### Make the scoring rule something two people can use

A clear rubric describes observable behavior. “Good notice” is too broad. “Correct room and date, current entrance instructions, no unconfirmed reopening promise” lets reviewers compare a draft against the same expectations. Mark both false statements and missing requirements.

Test the rubric on two outputs with another reviewer. If one reviewer counts a missing entrance as minor and the other calls it a failure, ask who needs that instruction and what happens without it. Revise the criterion so someone new can apply it consistently. Keep the original disagreement as useful evidence about where judgment varies.

Preserve the denominator and which requirements were applicable. A draft cannot be credited for avoiding a particular error if the task never tested that condition. Use a separate record for cases that were reviewed but could not be resolved from the source. Those cases may teach more about the workflow than a score alone.

## Worked example

A draft preserves the event date and claims a replacement venue without a source. Record a pass on the date and a fail on the unsupported venue.

### Follow the example

**Given:** A notice gives the right room but omits that the accessible entrance has moved.

**Walkthrough:** Score the room and the entrance separately. Record the omitted access instruction as material because it changes whether an attendee can use the notice.

## Try it yourself

A summary correctly reports the deadline and leaves out a required approval. Score the two details separately and decide whether to send it.

??? success "Compare your answer"
    The deadline passes. The omission fails the approval check. Revise before sending because the omitted step changes what the audience may do.
