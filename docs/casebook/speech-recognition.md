# Who gets left out of a transcript?

## What researchers measured

Koenecke and coauthors tested five commercial speech-recognition systems and reported average word error rates of 35% for Black speakers and 19% for white speakers in their benchmark. This is a result for the systems, speakers, recordings, and measurement used in that 2020 study. Treat it as a reason to inspect performance by group in a new use case. [Racial disparities in automated speech recognition](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1915768117)

## What the aggregate cannot tell a reviewer

Suppose a transcript changes “we did **not** approve the closure” to “we did approve the closure.” The surrounding passage is almost entirely correct. A word error measure will count a small edit. A person publishing the meeting decision needs to know that the meaning reversed.

For a fictional public meeting archive, review three different outcomes.

1. **Words** · Record how much text needs correction and identify recording or speaker patterns.
2. **Meaning** · Check decisions, dates, quantities, names, and negation against the audio.
3. **Recovery** · Check whether a reviewer can find the supporting audio and correct the result without replaying an entire meeting.

A product review could set a separate gate for official decisions while allowing a rough transcript to support discovery. It could also send uncertain excerpts to review and show the original time range. Think about the person whose comment is missing. An aggregate word error rate cannot show whether the omitted material came from one speaker repeatedly or was scattered across participants.
