# What changed for customer support workers

## What researchers observed

In a November 2023 working-paper revision, Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond analyze a staggered deployment of a conversational assistant for 5,179 customer support agents. They report an average increase of 14% in issues resolved per hour. Their estimate is considerably larger for novice and lower-skilled workers and much smaller for highly experienced workers. The tool provided guidance during conversations. Workers still handled the customer interaction. [Generative AI at Work, November 2023 revision](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w31161/w31161.pdf?LanguageId=1)

## Think about the person using it

A new agent can use a good suggestion as a shortcut to procedures an experienced colleague already knows. That gives a plausible reason for different outcomes by experience level, while the field result alone cannot tell another support team what lift to expect. Transfers are especially uncertain if the new team uses the assistant for different tasks or moves review work to a separate queue.

Imagine your team records these **illustrative** averages over comparable shifts. A manager has to decide whether to expand the pilot.

| Group | Manual issues resolved per hour | Assisted issues resolved per hour | Assisted cases returned for correction |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| New hires | 4.0 | 5.2 | 3 of 50 |
| Experienced agents | 7.0 | 7.1 | 8 of 50 |

Ask for the manual return rates and comparable case mixes before deciding. The table tells you how many assisted cases came back. It gives no baseline for the change in correction rates. If returns require long specialist reviews, tally that time as part of operating cost.

A reasonable pilot decision might keep assisted guidance for new-hire routine cases and continue measuring correction and escalation work. Give the team a way to decline suggestions and check whether staff are learning to solve common tasks independently.
